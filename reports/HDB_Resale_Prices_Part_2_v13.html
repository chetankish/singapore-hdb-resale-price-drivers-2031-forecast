Singapore HDB Resale Price Drivers and 2031 Forecast

Part 2: Regression Analysis and 2031 Forecasting

Introduction

After completing Part 1, I had a clearer view of the HDB resale market. The simple projection gave me a useful starting point, but the sensitivity check and segment analysis also showed that resale price is not affected by time alone.

In Part 2, I use what I learned from Part 1 to look for a better way to forecast HDB resale flat prices. The earlier analysis showed that resale price is not explained by time alone, so the next step is to test how different regression models handle price changes and whether they can give a more useful 2031 estimate.

To guide the modelling choices, I reviewed common housing price forecasting and property price modelling approaches, then focused on regression-based methods that fit my dataset: linear regression, semi-log regression, time-trend regression, a lagged dependent variable model and hedonic regression. I do not treat these models as a race to find the biggest forecast number. I use them step by step to understand what each model captures, what it misses and how the forecast changes when more information is added.

Model notation note: β1 represents the intercept and u represents the error term. For categorical variables, one reference category is omitted so that the regression can be estimated properly.

Project Files and Reproducibility

This modelling report should be read together with Part 1. The project code, SQL workflow and source notes are available in the GitHub repository. The official HDB resale collection is available on data.gov.sg. Because the official dataset continues to update, the repository documents the frozen data version used for this forecast.

The main modelling scripts are 02_part2_regression_models.py and 03_backtesting_and_diagnostics.py.

Time-Based Regression Models

I start with time-based regression models because they are the closest extension of the simple projection in Part 1. Instead of only applying a historical growth rate, these models estimate a relationship between resale price and time. This gives me a clearer baseline before I use models that include flat characteristics.

Model 1: Raw Linear Time Trend Regression

My first regression is deliberately simple. I use a raw linear time-trend regression because linear regression is a common first step in housing price forecasting: it measures the relationship between price and a chosen factor. In this model, the chosen factor is year, so it also works as a time-trend regression. This helps me check whether annual median HDB resale prices have a clear upward trend over time before I add other housing characteristics.

Price_t = β1 + β2 Year_t + u_t

The fitted line rises steadily, but Figure 1 shows that the resale market did not move in a simple straight line. The model has a high R squared of 0.896, the year coefficient is statistically significant with p < 0.001, and the t statistic is 17.073. However, the 2031 forecast is only S$632,155, which is barely above the 2025 median price of S$628,000. This tells me that the raw model may be too flat for the recent trend, even though it looks statistically strong in-sample.

Autocorrelation means the model errors are related across years, which can affect standard errors, t statistics and p values. This is another reason why I should not treat the raw linear time-trend regression as the final forecasting model.

Model 2: Semi-Log Regression

After checking the raw model, I next use the natural log of annual median resale price. Semi-log regression is commonly used in housing price forecasting because property prices often grow in percentage terms rather than by the same dollar amount every year. For example, a S$20,000 increase means something different when the median price is S$100,000 compared with when it is S$600,000. Using log price makes the model closer to compound growth and makes the results easier to interpret as percentage changes.

ln(Price_t) = β1 + β2 Year_t + u_t

The 2031 forecast is S$932,653, which is broadly close to the Part 1 mechanical projection of S$961,004, although slightly lower. The R squared should not be compared too directly with the raw model because the dependent variable is now log price. The main value of this model is that it treats price growth more like compound growth rather than the same dollar increase every year.

Model 3: Lagged Semi-Log Regression

After the semi-log model, I still need to deal with the fact that prices are linked across time. Lagged dependent variable models are commonly used in housing price forecasting because this year’s price is often influenced by last year’s price. For HDB resale flats, the 2025 median price may be partly affected by the price level in 2024, especially when there is strong market momentum. To try to reduce autocorrelation, I include the previous year’s log median resale price.

ln(Price_t) = β1 + β2 Year_t + β3 ln(Price_(t-1)) + u_t

In the lagged semi-log model, the lagged price coefficient is statistically significant, but the year coefficient is not statistically significant at conventional levels because its p value is 0.156. This suggests that once last year’s price is included, the separate linear year trend becomes less important. For the 2031 forecast, I use the model recursively from 2026 to 2031, because each forecast year depends on the previous year’s predicted price.

Backtesting the Time-Based Models

After comparing the time-based models, I identified the lagged semi-log model as the strongest time-based candidate because it had better fit, lower errors and reduced the autocorrelation problem compared with the earlier models. However, a model should not be judged only by how well it fits past data. To check whether it can forecast more realistically, I use backtesting. This means I train the model using earlier data, then test whether it can predict later years that are already known. This helps me assess the model's forecasting accuracy before using it for the 2031 estimate.

For this backtest, I train the time-based models using data only up to 2018 and then forecast 2019 to 2025. This tests actual forecasting performance rather than only in-sample fit.

Because the holdout period is only seven years, I treat the backtest as a near-term accuracy check rather than a full test of long-range 2031 reliability. This is why I consider both error metrics and model assumptions when interpreting the final forecast.

The backtest shows that the raw linear time-trend regression had the lowest dollar errors over the 2019 to 2025 holdout period, while all three models captured the upward direction of prices. However, this does not automatically make the raw model the best final forecasting model. The backtest period is short and the raw model still has visible autocorrelation and a very flat 2031 forecast. The result is useful because it shows that a model can perform well over a short test window but still be weak for longer range forecasting if its assumptions are too simple.

The backtest should therefore be treated as a useful check, not as proof that any model can predict 2031 accurately. It only tests performance over one recent holdout period and does not account for future policy changes, interest rates, income growth, supply conditions or shifts in transaction mix.

Regression Diagnostics and Interpretation

I use the diagnostics to check whether the models are useful, not just whether they produce a forecast number. R squared and adjusted R squared show how much variation the model explains, while RMSE and MAE show the typical forecast error in dollar terms.

The p values and t statistics help check whether individual coefficients are statistically different from zero. The F statistic checks whether the model is useful overall. However, statistical significance alone is not enough because a model can still give a poor forecast if its assumptions are weak.

The residual plots are important because they show whether the errors look random. In the raw and semi-log models, the residuals show autocorrelation, meaning the errors are linked across years. The lagged model reduces this problem, but it does not remove it completely because the Durbin Watson statistic is still below 2.

Multicollinearity is not relevant for the simple time-based regressions because they only have one independent variable. It is relevant for the hedonic regression because that model includes several related variables, such as flat type, flat model and floor area. To check this, I use the Variance Inflation Factor, or VIF. As a general rule, VIF values below 5 are usually acceptable, while values above 10 suggest serious multicollinearity. Values between 5 and 10 should be interpreted carefully rather than treated as automatic failure. In simple terms, a high VIF means that one explanatory variable overlaps strongly with other explanatory variables, which can make the individual coefficient harder to interpret.

Hedonic Regression

The time-based regressions are useful because they show the broad price trend, but they only use time. This is still too limited because HDB resale prices also differ by flat type, town, floor area, flat model, storey range and remaining lease. I chose hedonic regression after reviewing common housing price methods and finding that it is widely used when prices depend on property characteristics rather than a single market trend. For HDB resale flats, this means I can analyse resale price while controlling for several flat characteristics at the same time.

ln(Price_i) = β1 + β2 Year_i + β3 FloorArea_i + β4 RemainingLease_i + town dummies + flat type dummies + storey range dummies + flat model dummies + u_i

The VIF results show what is good and what needs caution in the hedonic model. Year and New Generation have VIF values below 5, so they are within the usually acceptable range. To reduce multicollinearity, I could test a simpler specification by removing or combining overlapping variables, grouping rare flat models, or comparing versions that use either flat type or floor area. For this project, I keep the hedonic model because these variables are important for explaining HDB resale prices, but I treat the VIF results as a warning when interpreting individual effects.

Final 2031 Forecast Summary

The overall median forecast and the controlled 4 ROOM forecast answer related but different questions. The overall forecast is based on annual median resale prices across all flat types, towns, sizes and lease profiles. The hedonic forecast is for a specific 93 sqm 4 ROOM Model A flat in selected towns, holding several characteristics constant. This is why the hedonic town forecasts can be much higher than the overall median range.

Final forecast interpretation. Overall median resale prices in 2031 are best interpreted as a scenario range rather than a point forecast. The time-based models and Part 1 baseline suggest a broad nominal range of about S$630,000 to S$960,000, with a more practical range of about S$700,000 to S$960,000 after downweighting the raw linear model. Separately, the hedonic model shows that a controlled 93 sqm 4 ROOM Model A flat could vary substantially by town, from about S$909,795 in Woodlands to about S$1,394,254 in Bukit Merah. These are nominal forecasts, not guaranteed future prices, and the result depends heavily on flat type, town, floor area, remaining lease and market conditions.

Stakeholder Implications and Areas to Monitor

The main value of this project is not just one 2031 number. The analysis also shows which parts of the resale market stakeholders should monitor more closely.

First, 4 ROOM flats should be monitored as a key mainstream segment because they are a common family flat type. Second, million-dollar transactions should be tracked by share rather than only by count, because counts can rise simply when total transaction volume rises.

Third, price per square metre should be used together with total resale price because raw prices are affected by floor area. Fourth, town should be treated as an important location signal, even though the dataset does not include exact distance to MRT stations, schools or amenities.

Finally, remaining lease should be included in serious resale analysis because HDB flats have finite 99-year leases. Forecasts should also be presented as ranges or scenarios rather than one exact number, because the result depends on modelling assumptions and future market conditions.

Limitations and Next Steps

The model choices were informed by common housing price forecasting and property price modelling approaches, but I only used methods that fit the available HDB resale dataset and project scope. Methods that require extra macroeconomic data, exact amenity distances, renovation quality or repeated sales tracking were not used here.

A useful next step would be to test reduced hedonic specifications, such as comparing models with either flat type or floor area, grouping rare flat models, and adding external features such as MRT proximity, interest rates, income growth or housing supply indicators if reliable data is available.
