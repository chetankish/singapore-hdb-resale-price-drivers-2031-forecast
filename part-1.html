Singapore HDB Resale Price Drivers and 2031 Forecast
Chetan
July 26, 2026
Abstract
This report examines long-run Singapore HDB resale price movements using official transaction data from 1990 to May 2026. It begins with a transparent baseline projection, then tests how sensitive the 2031 estimate is to the chosen historical starting point. The analysis then explains the data preparation workflow and compares price patterns by flat type, town, floor area, remaining lease and million-dollar transactions. The purpose is to show not only what changed in the resale market, but also why each analytical step was needed before moving into controlled modelling.

Part 1

Introduction

As a Singaporean, the idea of owning a home one day is more than just a financial decision. It is a life milestone. In recent years, I kept seeing more news about HDB resale flats crossing the S$1 million mark. This made me wonder what the resale market might look like by the time I reach the stage of buying a home.

This question connects to my own timeline. I am expecting to graduate in two years, and ideally would like to work for another two to three years before seriously considering a home purchase. This places the possibility of my earliest buying window in about 2031. At first, my question seemed simple: how much might I be looking to pay for an HDB resale flat in 2031?

However, this personal question also connects to a broader business and policy problem. HDB resale prices do not only matter to individual buyers. They are also important to housing analysts, policymakers, financial institutions and other stakeholders who need to understand where affordability pressure is coming from, which market segments are driving price growth, and what future resale prices could look like under different assumptions.

Therefore, this project uses HDB resale transaction data to answer both a personal and stakeholder focused question: how have HDB resale prices changed across time, flat type, town and remaining lease, and what does this suggest about resale affordability pressure by 2031?

To answer this, I break the analysis into three supporting questions:

  1. Where is resale price pressure coming from?
  2. Which market segments should stakeholders monitor?
  3. What could resale prices look like by 2031 under different modelling assumptions?

Initial Baseline Projection

My first thought was to use a simple growth based projection because it seemed like a good place to start. It gives me a rough idea of what 2031 prices could look like if past growth continues, and I can see how useful that simple approach is before deciding what else needs to be checked.

The dataset used in this report contains 977,756 HDB resale transactions from January 1990 to May 2026. However, 2026 is incomplete, so I do not use it as a complete year in the main annual trend charts. Including it directly could give readers the wrong impression that the 2026 median resale price is a full year figure. The 2026 median resale price so far is S$630,000, but this only reflects transactions up to May 2026, so it is shown only as context where relevant.

Figure 1 shows that HDB resale prices increased strongly over the long run, from a median resale price of S$52,500 in 1990 to S$628,000 in 2025. However, the increase was not smooth. The early 1990s show a strong rise, which is consistent with rapid economic growth and strong housing demand during that period. The late 1990s then show a correction, which provides context for the Asian Financial Crisis and weaker market confidence.

After that, resale prices moved through several cycles. Some periods were flatter or weaker, especially after cooling measures and tighter financing rules affected the resale market. More recently, prices rose sharply after COVID related construction delays, strong resale demand and tight supply conditions. These events do not prove direct causation by themselves, but they help explain why the price trend moved in cycles rather than in a straight line.

From 1990 to 2025, the annual median HDB resale price increased from S$52,500 to S$628,000. This is a total increase of about 1,096.2%. Annualising this over 35 years gives an annualised growth rate of about 7.35% per year.

If I use this 7.35% annualised growth rate as a simple mechanical projection and compound the 2025 median resale price of S$628,000 for another six years, the estimated 2031 median resale price would be about S$961,004.

After calculating this, I realised that the projection is only a mechanical baseline. It assumes that future economic conditions, housing policies, interest rates, income growth, supply, demand and transaction mix remain broadly similar to the long run historical period. That is a strong assumption, so I should not treat this result as the final answer.

To check whether this rough projection is stable, I look at what happens when the annualised growth rate is calculated from different starting years. This matters because the 1990 starting point may not represent every market condition. If the starting year is unusually low or unusually high, the 2031 estimate can change significantly.

The simple 1990 to 2025 projection gives a rough baseline, but it depends heavily on the starting year. By repeating the calculation from different starting years, I can test whether the 2031 estimate is stable or whether it changes when the starting point changes.

Understanding HDB Flat Types and Flat Models

Before comparing the market segments, I first need to clarify two terms used in the dataset. HDB flat type and flat model are not the same. Flat type is the broad size category, while flat model describes the design or generation label used in the resale records. This distinction matters because the same flat type can appear under different flat models.

Table 3. HDB flat type categories used in the report

Flat type How it should be read
2 ROOM / 2 ROOM Flexi Smaller flats usually suited to singles, seniors or smaller households.
3 ROOM Compact family flat category. Lower raw price does not always mean lower price per sqm.
4 ROOM A common family flat category and the main benchmark used later in the report.
5 ROOM Larger family flat category. Raw prices are usually higher partly because the units are larger.
Executive Large flat category. It can have high raw prices, but price per sqm must still be checked.

The flat_model column gives more detail about the design or layout category of the flat. This is different from flat_type. For example, two flats can both be 4 ROOM flats, but they may have different models, layouts, floor areas or design periods. This matters because flat model can affect resale price together with flat type, town, floor area and remaining lease.

Table 4. Common HDB flat model labels used in the resale data

Flat modelHow to explain it in the report

The main point is that flat_model helps capture differences that are not fully explained by flat_type alone. However, some flat model labels overlap with flat type, floor area and flat age, so individual flat model effects should be interpreted carefully in later modelling work.

The official HDB data collection was not provided as one single file. Instead, the collection is split into five historical CSV files covering different time periods. In this report, I treat the month column as the transaction period, while noting this as a data consistency limitation.

Baseline Forecasting Assumptions

For the forecast, I use a baseline scenario. This means I assume that the main conditions affecting HDB resale prices remain broadly similar to recent years. These assumptions make the forecast easier to interpret.

1. Economic conditions: I assume that Singapore economy remains stable, with no major recession or financial crisis.

2. Interest rates: I assume that mortgage rates remain within a reasonable range and do not rise sharply.

3. Income growth: I assume that household incomes continue to grow gradually.

4. Inflation: I assume that inflation remains moderate and does not create a major affordability shock.

5. Housing policy: I assume that HDB resale rules, grants, CPF usage and loan limits remain broadly similar.

6. Housing supply: I assume that BTO supply, resale supply and the number of flats reaching MOP remain broadly similar to recent years.

7. Buyer demand: I assume that demand from eligible resale buyers remains stable.

8. Transaction mix: I assume that the mix of flats sold remains broadly similar across flat type, town, age and size.

9. External shocks: I assume that there is no major external shock, such as another pandemic, global financial crisis or sudden supply disruption.

When inspecting the files, I noticed that the column structure was not fully consistent across all periods. Some newer files had additional columns that older files did not have. One important example was remaining lease, which was missing from older records.

This mattered because remaining lease is an important factor in resale value. Most HDB flats are sold on 99 year leases, so for older records where remaining lease was missing, I estimated it using the transaction year and the lease commencement year with SQL.

Market Overview: What Changed Over Time?

To properly understand and forecast HDB resale prices, I need to understand what factors may be affecting them. After the simple projection, I check whether the market movement can be explained by one broad trend or whether different measures reveal different patterns. Raw price shows what buyers paid. Price per square metre adjusts for flat size. Transaction volume shows market activity. Annual growth highlights unusually strong or weak years. Million dollar sales connect the data back to the public affordability debate. Each measure is used because the previous single measure is not enough on its own.

By 2025, the median resale price index had risen to about 1196. The median price per square metre index rose to about 925. The gap between the two lines suggests that total resale price and price per square metre did not increase at the same rate, so price growth cannot be understood from time alone.

Figure 4 shows actual median price per square metre rather than an index. It rose from about S$703 in 1990 to S$6,500 in 2025, confirming that the long term increase remains clear even after adjusting for floor area. This is why price per sqm is used alongside raw resale price: it helps compare flats more fairly when floor area differs.

Transaction volume helps show how active the resale market was and can also highlight unusual years. The late 1990s had very high activity, while some later periods were quieter. These movements provide market context and should not be interpreted as causal proof on their own.

Annual growth shows which years were unusually strong or weak. The sharp movements around the 1990s and later correction periods provide context for why a simple straight line forecast may not be sufficient.

In 2025, resale transactions at S$1 million or more formed 6.35% of all 2025 resale transactions in the workbook.

The first HDB resale flat to reach the million dollar mark was not from Pinnacle@Duxton, which many people may assume because Pinnacle@Duxton is now strongly associated with high value resale flats. The first million dollar HDB resale happened in 2012 at 149 Mei Ling Street in Queenstown, where a 150 sqm executive apartment was sold for S$1 million.

This was surprising because HDB flats are public housing and are generally expected to remain affordable for ordinary households. However, the transaction also shows that million dollar resale prices are usually not random. They tend to involve special combinations of location, flat size, flat type, rarity, storey level, remaining lease and buyer demand. The second million dollar HDB resale also happened in 2012, which makes 2012 the starting point of a trend that later became much more visible in the resale market. For stakeholders, the main takeaway is not only that million dollar flats exist, but that they should be tracked by share and by segment to understand whether high price pressure is spreading beyond a few unusual cases.

Inflation and CPI: Did Prices Rise Only Because Everything Became More Expensive?

I used CPI because I wanted to separate general inflation from HDB resale specific price growth. If resale prices increased mainly because everything in the economy became more expensive, then the increase would look closer to the CPI increase. However, if HDB resale prices grew much faster than CPI, it suggests that other housing market factors may also be driving the change, such as flat type, town, supply, demand, remaining lease and transaction mix.

Nominal prices show what buyers paid at the time. However, they do not show whether flats became more expensive after accounting for the general rise in prices across the economy. CPI adjustment does not prove how much of HDB price growth was caused by inflation. It only shows what HDB prices look like after adjusting for general consumer price inflation.

This answers the first supporting question: resale price pressure is not coming from time alone. It appears across several areas, including higher price per sqm, stronger million dollar transaction share, differences by flat type, town effects and remaining lease differences.

What Drives Price Differences Between HDB Resale Flats?

After looking at the overall market trend, the next question is why two HDB flats sold in the same year can have very different prices. This section compares flat type, town and remaining lease because these are practical market segments that stakeholders can monitor. The current dataset does not directly include exact distances to MRT stations, schools, malls or other amenities, so town is used as a broad location proxy.

Flat Type: Why Raw Price Can Mislead

Figure 9 shows that 4 ROOM flats make up the largest share of resale transactions. This matters because 4 ROOM flats are a practical benchmark for the mainstream resale market. They are large enough for many families, but usually more affordable than 5 ROOM or executive flats. Their high transaction share likely reflects both supply and demand: there are many 4 ROOM flats in the overall HDB stock, and many buyers are also looking for this flat type in the resale market.

The low number of 2 ROOM transactions is also not surprising, but it should be interpreted carefully. Figure 9 uses the 2 ROOM resale category in the dataset, so it does not separately isolate every 2 ROOM Flexi arrangement. In general, smaller two room flats serve a narrower buyer group because they are more suitable for singles, seniors or smaller households. Separately, some short lease 2 ROOM Flexi flats are meant mainly for seniors and cannot be sold on the open resale market. This means they do not appear as normal resale supply, which helps explain why small two room flats are much less visible in resale transactions than 4 ROOM flats.

Figure 10 shows an interesting result. In theory, 2 ROOM flats may be expected to look cheaper because they are smaller and are often meant to serve lower income households, seniors and singles. However, the chart shows that 2 ROOM flats have the highest median price per square metre.

This does not mean 2 ROOM flats are the most expensive in total price. Instead, it shows that smaller flats can have higher prices when measured on a per square metre basis. A smaller flat spreads its total price over fewer square metres, so the price per sqm can look high even if the total resale price is lower than larger flat types.

This result is surprising because 2 ROOM flats are usually expected to be more affordable in total price. This is likely influenced by the smaller sample size of 2 ROOM transactions, differences in location, lease profile and the types of units included in the resale market. Since smaller flats divide total price across fewer square metres, a small number of higher priced transactions can have a larger effect on the median price per sqm. This result would require a separate analysis of flat age, location and buyer segment differences, so I focus on the broader resale price drivers in this report.

This comparison shows why raw resale price can mislead. Larger flats can have higher total prices because they have more floor area, even when their price per square metre is not the highest. For stakeholders, price per sqm is useful because it separates the price of space from the total price of the unit.

Town and Location: Why Area Matters

Figure 11 shows that flats in central or mature towns tend to have higher median price per square metre. These towns are often more developed and better connected. Since the dataset does not directly measure MRT distance, school proximity or nearby amenities, town is used as a practical location signal. This is a general pattern rather than a rule that applies to every flat, and newer estates such as Punggol can also be sought after because of newer flats and estate development.

This town comparison also helps explain why some flats can reach unusually high prices. Million dollar HDB transactions are not random. They are usually linked to special combinations of location, flat size, flat type, storey level, remaining lease and rarity. Therefore, the first million dollar HDB resale should be understood as an extreme example of these price drivers coming together, rather than as a normal resale transaction.

Figure 12 shows that remaining lease still matters even when the flat type, floor area and flat model are kept broadly comparable. This measure is used because HDB flats have finite 99 year leases. Flats with longer remaining leases generally have higher resale values because buyers are paying for a longer period of use. As the remaining lease gets shorter, the flat may become less attractive to some buyers, especially if CPF usage, loan eligibility or long term holding value becomes a concern.

This also shows why age should not be ignored when comparing resale prices. Two flats may look similar in size and flat type, but the one with a longer remaining lease can still command a higher price. Therefore, remaining lease should be included in the forecasting model later, rather than relying only on year, town or flat type.

Remaining lease is important because most HDB flats start with a 99 year lease. A flat with more lease remaining gives buyers a longer housing horizon and may have a wider buyer pool. The controlled example is illustrative, not a final forecast.

This answers the second supporting question. Based on the segment analysis, stakeholders should monitor 4 ROOM flats as a mainstream resale segment, million dollar transactions by share, town level price differences and remaining lease bands.

What This Report Has Established

Part 1 shows the logic behind the analysis rather than only chasing a final forecast number. I first tested a simple growth based projection because it gives an easy baseline. I then checked how sensitive that projection was to the chosen starting year, which showed why a single mechanical forecast should not be treated as final.

The analysis then moved into better measures and market segments. Price per sqm was used because raw resale price is affected by floor area. Million dollar transactions were studied by share because counts alone can be affected by total transaction volume. Town was used as a practical location proxy because exact amenity distances were not available. Remaining lease was included because HDB flats have finite 99 year leases.

Overall, Part 1 answers the three supporting questions at a descriptive level. It shows where resale price pressure is coming from, which market segments are worth monitoring, and how sensitive a simple 2031 projection can be. This creates the foundation for Part 2, where I use regression models to test the price relationships more formally and produce a more controlled forecast.

This prepares the ground for Part 2, where the focus shifts from descriptive analysis to regression based forecasting. The cleaned annual series can support time based models, while the cleaned transaction level data can support hedonic regression. The main value of the project is the structured analytical workflow: prepare the data, test a simple baseline, identify its weaknesses, use better measures, and then move into more controlled forecasting.
